# Additional Skills

In addition to the core skills required for a job, there are often additional skills that can enhance your qualifications and make you a more competitive candidate. These skills may not be explicitly listed in the job description but can still be valuable in the workplace. Here are some examples of additional skills that can be beneficial:

I am eligible for security clearance and have previously held TS/SCI. Jobs that require active clearance are blockers, but those that require eligibility are not. I have experience with the clearance process and can provide documentation if needed.

I can meet physical requirements such as lifting up to 50 pounds, standing for long periods, or working in confined spaces, climbing ladders, working in hazardous environments. I have no physical limitations that would prevent me from performing the duties of the job.

R, SQL, Agile, Spark, Hadoop, AWS, Azure, Snowflake, pytorch, kubernetes, typescript (TS) can all be acceptable if the requirement is soft, under 3 years experience and not a core requirement. For example, if a job requires 5 years of experience with R, but I have 2 years of experience and the requirement is listed as "preferred" rather than "required," I would still consider applying for the position. However, if the job requires 5 years of experience with R and it is listed as a "required" skill, I would likely not meet the qualifications for that role.

Any type of simulation, predictive modeling, etc are fine.

Management jobs requiring less than 4 years of experience with small teams are fine due to the phd work.

## How to Add a Skill to Your Application Materials

When tailoring your resume and cover letter for a new role, you may want to add or emphasize skills that are not prominently featured in your base resume. Here is how to do it truthfully and effectively:

### 1. Only Add Skills You Actually Have

- **Hard requirement:** Every skill you list must be backed by real project experience, professional work, coursework, or documented practice.
- **Never fabricate.** Adding a skill you don't have is grounds for immediate disqualification if discovered during a technical interview or background check.
- **Threshold:** You should have at least basic competency — enough to discuss the skill in an interview without being caught out.

### 2. Where to Add or Emphasize Skills

Skills can be surfaced in three places on your resume:

#### Capsule Statement (Headline)
- Add 3-4 hard skills drawn from the job description and your actual background.
- Use only skills that are core to the role and appear multiple times in the posting.
- Example: If the job is for "Senior Data Analyst" and you know SQL and Python well, include both in the headline.

#### Skills Section
- List 15-30 hard skills in a comma-separated format (no bullet points).
- Prioritize skills that match the job posting's requirements.
- Reorder this section for each application to put the most relevant skills first.
- **Do not include soft skills** (leadership, communication, teamwork, etc.) — those belong in bullet points.

#### Bullet Points Under Relevant Experience
- Naturally mention the most relevant hard skills when describing past projects.
- Example: Instead of "Managed reporting," write "Developed Power BI dashboards automating reporting and saving 10+ hours weekly."
- This signals relevance without being obvious or robotic.

### 3. Truthful Emphasis Without Fabrication

You can surface existing skills more prominently without lying:

- **Reorder skills:** If you know Python but haven't used it in 2 years, you can still list it — but place it lower in the skills section for a role that doesn't heavily emphasize Python.
- **Emphasize projects:** If you have used a skill in a side project, research context, or early career role, you can include it. Just be ready to discuss it.
- **Be specific about context:** In a bullet point, you can write "Python (scripting and data analysis)" to clarify depth if the skill context varies.

### 4. Validation Checklist Before Submission

Before adding a skill to your application:

- [ ] Can you demonstrate this skill in a technical interview? 
- [ ] Have you used it in a professional project, research role, or documented practice?
- [ ] Does the job posting actually ask for or value this skill?
- [ ] Can you point to a specific project or timeframe when you used it?
- [ ] Is the skill level (beginner, intermediate, advanced) accurately represented in how you present it?

### 5. Examples of Truthful Skill Surfacing

**Example 1: Academic Background**
- Job posting asks for "Machine Learning" experience.
- You did machine learning coursework and thesis work in your PhD.
- Action: Include "Machine Learning" in your skills section and mention your thesis project in a relevant bullet point.
- What NOT to do: Don't claim you have 5+ years of professional ML experience if you only have academic exposure.

**Example 2: Soft Skills Misplaced**
- Job posting emphasizes "Team Leadership."
- You managed a small research team as part of your PhD.
- Action: Add a bullet point about leading the team effort; do NOT put "Leadership" in the hard skills section.
- What NOT to do: Don't list "Leadership" as a hard technical skill — it belongs in a description, not the skills list.

**Example 3: Secondary Tool with Real Usage**
- Job posting requires SQL as a must-have and "Python preferred."
- You know Python well but haven't used it professionally for the last year (you switched to R).
- Action: List Python in the skills section but place SQL first. Mention a Python project in a bullet point if relevant.
- What NOT to do: Don't remove Python entirely if you actually know it — just reorder to match priorities.

### 6. Red Flags to Avoid

- Adding a skill with no way to back it up in an interview.
- Claiming years of experience you don't have ("5 years of X" when you have 6 months).
- Listing soft skills in the hard skills section.
- Adding niche or highly technical skills without mentioning the project context.
- Contradicting the skills section within your bullet points (saying SQL is a core skill, then never mentioning it in your work descriptions).

---

**Golden Rule:** Your resume and cover letter should make it easy for you to succeed in a phone screening or technical interview. Every claim should be something you can defend with a concrete example. If you can't, don't add it.
